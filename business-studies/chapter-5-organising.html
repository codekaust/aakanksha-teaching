Chapter 5
Organising

A plan tells you what is going to be done. It does not tell you who will do it, what he is allowed to decide, or whom he answers to when it goes wrong. Organising answers all three.

Where we were

From Chapter 4 — Planning

Chapter 4 produced a plan. A plan names what will be done; organising decides who does it, with what authority, answering to whom.

Planning ends with a decision on paper. Nothing has moved yet. The moment you ask "who will actually do this?" you have left planning and entered organising — the second function in the cycle.

The one line to remember

Organising is the function that translates plans into action. Without it a plan is only an intention with a deadline attached.

Mark weight

Units 4 and 5 together carry 14 marks of the 80-mark paper. Organising is asked most often as a case study — "which structure should this company adopt, and why", or "identify the concept the manager has ignored".

What organising actually means

Start with something you have watched happen.

Example — the school annual day

Doon Valley Public School, Dehradun, decides to hold its annual day on 12 December. Nobody does the whole thing. The work splits into a stage and decoration committee, a ticketing committee, a refreshments committee and a discipline committee. Each committee gets a teacher-in-charge. Every teacher-in-charge reports to the Vice Principal, who reports to the Principal.

Dividing the work, grouping it, giving each group a head, and fixing who reports to whom — that is organising. You have already seen it a hundred times.

Definition — exam wording

Organising is the process of identifying and grouping the work to be performed, defining and delegating responsibility and authority, and establishing relationships for the purpose of enabling people to work most effectively together in accomplishing objectives. — Louis Allen

The word is used in two senses — and the question tells you which

Organising as a PROCESS

The steps a manager follows: divide the work, group it into departments, assign the duties, fix the reporting relationships.

Asked as: "Explain the steps in the process of organising."

Organising as a STRUCTURE

The framework those steps produce — the chart of departments, posts and reporting lines that the firm then runs on.

Asked as: "What is organisation structure? Explain its types."

Read the verb

"Organising" = the process. "Organisation structure" = the output. A student who writes the four steps when the question asked for the types of structure has written a correct answer to a question nobody set.

The process of organising — four steps

How to read this
  1. Read top to bottom — the numbered discs are the exam order, and marks are cut for reordering them.
  2. Each arrow means "only after this is done" — you cannot group work you have not yet divided.
  3. The amber box is the one students forget: allocating work is not enough, the reporting line must be fixed.
  4. The small grey line inside each box is the one-line explanation the examiner wants after the heading.

The four steps in one firm

Case 1 · Kathiawar Ceramics, Rajkot

Hasmukh Vora runs a 140-worker ceramics unit making floor tiles. In April 2026 he adds a sanitaryware line. He lists everything the new line needs — clay preparation, casting, glazing, firing, quality checking, packing, despatch. He then puts casting, glazing and firing together under a single Production section, and quality checking and packing under a separate Finishing section. Ramesh Solanki, who has fired kilns for eleven years, is made Kiln Supervisor. Every supervisor is told to report to the Works Manager, who reports to Hasmukh.

Question 1 4 marks

Identify and explain the four steps in the process of organising that Hasmukh has followed, quoting the line from the case for the second step.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. Identification and division of workhe listed clay preparation, casting, glazing, firing, quality checking, packing and despatch — the total work broken into manageable activities.
  2. Departmentalisationactivities similar in nature were grouped into a Production section and a Finishing section.
  3. Assignment of dutiesRamesh Solanki, with eleven years of kiln experience, was made Kiln Supervisor — the job matched to the ability.
  4. Establishing reporting relationshipsevery supervisor reports to the Works Manager, who reports to Hasmukh — this creates the hierarchy.
…puts casting, glazing and firing together under a single Production section, and quality checking and packing under a separate Finishing section.

That sentence is the whole mark for step 2. Naming the step without quoting the line is a half answer.

Points ≈ marks — how to size an answer

Say this once, use it all year

A Business Studies answer is never a paragraph. It is a numbered list of headings, each followed by one or two lines. And the number of points is set by the mark:

MarksPointsWhat the examiner is counting
1 mark1 linethe term, or one sentence — no explanation needed
3 marks3 pointsheading + one line each
4 marks4 pointsheading + one or two lines each
5–6 marks5–6 pointsheading + explanation, and in a case, the quoted line
 A heading alone earns roughly half the point. An unheaded paragraph earns less than the heading alone.
The commonest avoidable loss

Writing six beautiful points for a 3-mark question and then running out of time in the 6-mark one. Size the answer to the tag on the question, not to how much you happen to know.

Importance of organising — seven points

Benefits of specialisation

Repeated performance of one job builds skill; work is systematically allocated and productivity rises.

Clarity in working relationships

Who reports to whom is specified, so instructions and information travel without ambiguity.

Optimum utilisation of resources

Duplication and overlapping of work are avoided, so material, financial and human resources are not wasted.

Adaptation to change

The structure can be modified when the business environment changes, giving the firm stability through the transition.

Effective administration

Clear job descriptions and clear relationships remove confusion, so management of the enterprise becomes easier.

Development of personnel

Delegation frees the manager from routine work to innovate, and develops the subordinate's ability to handle challenges.

Expansion and growth

New posts, departments, product lines and territories can be added without disturbing existing operations.

Hook Specialisation · Clarity · Optimum use · Adaptation · Effective administration · Development · Expansion

The hook only orders the list. In the paper each point still needs its one-line explanation — the hook alone scores nothing.

Farhan Qureshi founded a 24-person advertising agency and kept it deliberately informal — "we are a family, not a chart". By 2026 the agency handles nine clients. Two copywriters spent a fortnight on the same Diwali campaign without knowing it, and a client's brief sat for six days because everyone assumed someone else had picked it up.   2. Clarity in working relationshipsnobody knew who was to pick up the brief, because reporting relationships were never established.

★ Challenge — planning or organising?

Case 3 · Kathiawar Ceramics, Rajkot

At the April review Hasmukh does two things in the same meeting. First he fixes the target: "we will make 4,000 wash basins a month by October and sell them through 60 dealers in Saurashtra." Then he says: "Ramesh will run the casting shop with two moulders under him and will report to the Works Manager every evening."

Challenge 4 marks

A student writes: "Both decisions are planning, because both were taken in advance." Say why that is wrong, and name the function each decision belongs to, with a reason.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. "Taken in advance" is not the testevery managerial function is performed before the event. The test is what the decision decides — an end, or the arrangement of people to reach it.
  2. The first decision is planning4,000 wash basins a month by October, sold through 60 dealers, is an objective — it fixes what is to be achieved and by when.
  3. The second decision is organisinggiving Ramesh two moulders and a reporting line to the Works Manager assigns duties and establishes authority and reporting relationships — steps 3 and 4 of the organising process.
  4. The relationship between the twoorganising has no content until planning has supplied a goal; planning stays on paper until organising supplies the people. Organising translates plans into action.

Organisation structure — the output of organising

Definition — exam wording

The organisation structure is the framework within which managerial and operating tasks are performed. It specifies the relationships between people, work and resources, allows coordination among human, physical and financial resources, and is depicted in an organisation chart.

Why a firm changes its structure

A structure is not permanent. As a firm grows in size or in complexity, new functions appear and hierarchies lengthen, and coordination becomes difficult. Peter Drucker: "organisation structure is an indispensable means; and the wrong structure will seriously impair business performance and even destroy it."

Example — the same firm, twice

Kathiawar Ceramics ran a Production–Marketing–Finance–HR chart for eleven years on one product. The moment it added sanitaryware, bath fittings and kitchen sinks, the same chart began to strain. Nothing about the firm's people changed — only the number of product lines.

Span of management — the number that decides the shape

Definition — exam wording

Span of management refers to the number of subordinates that can be effectively managed by a superior. It determines the number of levels of management in the structure — and therefore the shape of the whole chart.

What decides the span

The nature of the work (routine work allows a wider span), the ability of the manager and of the subordinates, and how well the work has been planned. There is no universally correct number.

Span of management in a school

Case 4 · Doon Valley Public School, Dehradun

Principal Prakash Rawat had all 46 teachers reporting directly to him. Leave applications waited a week for his signature, and he had time to observe each teacher's class only once a year. In 2026 he created four Head-of-Department posts — Languages, Science, Commerce and Humanities — with roughly eleven teachers under each, all four reporting to a new Vice Principal.

Question 3 3 marks

Name the concept the Principal has applied, state what it means, and explain what happened to the shape of the school's structure.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. The concept is span of managementthe number of subordinates who can be effectively managed by one superior.
  2. His span was too wide to be effective46 direct subordinates meant week-long delays and one class observation a year — supervision existed on the chart but not in fact.
  3. The structure became tallernarrowing the span to eleven added two levels — Vice Principal and Heads of Department — so the chart is now four levels deep instead of two.
Leave applications waited a week for his signature, and he had time to observe each teacher's class only once a year.

The word "effectively" in the definition is doing all the work. He could manage 46 people; he could not manage them effectively.

Two types of organisation structure

Every organisation chart in the syllabus is one of exactly two kinds. The whole question is: what did you group the jobs by?

Not a third type

Divisional does not replace functional — it contains it. Inside every division you will find a functional structure again, because a division still has to produce, market and account for its product. Students who write "a divisional structure has no functions" lose the mark.

Functional structure

Definition — exam wording

A functional structure is one in which jobs of a similar nature are grouped together and organised as separate departments — production, purchase, marketing, finance and personnel — each headed by a functional head, and all departments report to a coordinating head such as the Managing Director.

Functional structure — advantages

  1. Occupational specialisationemployees perform similar tasks within a department every day, so skill and efficiency in that function rise.
  2. Better control and coordination within a departmentthe tasks inside one department are alike, so a functional head can control them easily.
  3. Increased managerial and operational efficiencywhich shows up directly as higher profit.
  4. Minimal duplication of effortthere is one Finance department, not one per product — this gives economies of scale and lowers cost.
  5. Easier training of employeesthe focus is on a limited range of skills, so a new recruit has less to learn.
  6. Due attention to every functioneach function has a head whose job is to defend it, so no function is quietly starved.
Example

Kathiawar Ceramics buys clay for the whole factory through one purchase desk. Buying 300 tonnes at a time instead of six lots of 50 tonnes cut its clay cost by about 8% in 2025-26.

Functional structure — disadvantages

  1. Functional empiresa department pursues its own objective rather than the overall enterprise objective, and the importance of one function gets over-emphasised.
  2. Problems in coordinationinformation has to be exchanged across functionally differentiated departments that do not speak the same language.
  3. Conflict of interestwhen two departments want incompatible things — the sales department insists on a customer-friendly design that production cannot make cheaply.
  4. Inflexibility and restricted managerial developmentpeople with the same skills develop a narrow perspective, and a functional head never gains experience in other areas, so he is not trained for a top management post.
Example

Kathiawar's Sales Manager promised a Bhavnagar builder a matt-finish tile in three weeks. The Works Manager, judged only on output per kiln, refused to break the running batch. The order was lost — and neither manager had done anything wrong by his own department's yardstick.

Functional structure — suitability

Write this sentence in the paper

A functional structure is most suitable when the size of the organisation is large, it has diversified activities, and operations require a high degree of specialisation.

"Diversified activities" means many different kinds of work — production, purchase, marketing, finance, personnel. It does not mean many different products. That distinction is the whole of the ⓘ panel below, and it decides every case question.

And add the discriminator the case questions actually turn on

The firm makes one product line, or a few closely related products. Once the products need different plants and different customers, the answer is divisional. Write the textbook sentence and this line — the first earns the recall mark, the second earns the application mark.

The test in one question: "could one Marketing department sensibly sell everything this firm makes?" If yes, functional. If not, divisional.

Example

Kathiawar Ceramics made only floor tiles for eleven years, in a plant of 140 workers. Large enough to need departments; single enough that one Marketing department could sell all of it. Functional structure was the right choice — until it added three more product lines.

"Diversified activities" here, "variety of products" there — is the book contradicting itself?

It looks like it, and this is a fair objection. The textbook says a functional structure suits a firm that is large and "has a diversified activities", and then says a divisional structure suits enterprises "where a large variety of products are manufactured". If those two meant the same thing, the chapter would be arguing against itself.

They do not mean the same thing. An activity is a kind of work; a product is a thing sold. A firm making one product can still carry a dozen diversified activities — purchasing, machining, assembly, quality control, advertising, credit control, recruitment — and it is exactly that variety of work which makes it worth creating a specialist department for each. Diversified products are a different fact, and that is the one the book uses to send you to a divisional structure.

So write the textbook sentence in the paper. It is what the marking scheme is keyed to, and dropping "diversified activities" from a recall answer risks the mark. Then add the discriminator — one product line, or a few closely related ones — because that is what a case question is actually testing.

The one-line safeguard: never write "diversified products" in a functional-structure answer. That single word swap is what turns a correct answer into evidence that you have confused the two structures.

Choosing a functional structure — a case

Case 5 · Kathiawar Ceramics, Rajkot

Before the 2026 expansion, Hasmukh employed 140 people making one thing: 30×30 cm floor tiles, in eleven colours. The same kiln fires every colour, the same six dealers sell every colour, and the same accountant costs every colour. He wants to know whether he should give each colour its own head.

Question 4 4 marks

Which structure should Hasmukh keep, and why? Give three advantages he would lose if he reorganised by colour.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. He should keep the functional structureeleven colours of one tile are a single product line, not diversified products — so grouping by function, not by product, is correct.
  2. He would lose economies of scaleduplicating a kiln, a sales desk and an accountant eleven times over multiplies cost for no gain.
  3. He would lose occupational specialisationhis kiln staff would be split into eleven thin teams instead of one deep one.
  4. He would lose ease of training and controlone production department with one set of skills is far simpler to train and supervise than eleven parallel ones.
The same kiln fires every colour, the same six dealers sell every colour, and the same accountant costs every colour.

Shared plant + shared customers + shared costing = one product line. That sentence is the examiner telling you the answer.

Divisional structure

Definition — exam wording

A divisional structure is one in which the organisation is divided into separate business units or divisions, each based on a product line. Each division is multifunctional — production, marketing, finance and purchase are all performed within it — is headed by a divisional manager responsible for its performance, and works as a profit centre.

Suryodaya before and after — one firm, two charts

Nothing about Suryodaya's people changed between these two pictures. Only the number of product lines did — and that alone rebuilt the chart.

Divisional structure — advantages

  1. Product specialisation and managerial developmentthe divisional head gains experience in all functions of one product line, which prepares him for higher positions — the very thing a functional head cannot get.
  2. Greater accountability and performance measurementrevenues and costs are identifiable division by division, so responsibility for profit can be fixed on the divisional head, and remedial action taken where a division underperforms.
  3. Flexibility and initiativeeach division is an autonomous unit, so decisions are taken faster and closer to the market.
  4. Facilitates expansion and growtha new product line can be added as a new division — one more head and one more team — without disturbing existing operations.
Example

When Suryodaya launched a fourth line, hand wash, in June 2026, it appointed one divisional head and one team. The soaps, detergents and shampoo divisions carried on without a single change to their targets — which is what "without interrupting existing operations" means in practice.

Divisional structure — disadvantages and suitability

  1. Conflict between divisionsover the allocation of funds, and a division may maximise its own profit at the cost of other divisions.
  2. Increase in costseach division has its own production, marketing and finance set-up, so activities are duplicated across products and expenditure rises.
  3. Ignoring organisational interestsa divisional manager with authority over everything in his division may, over time, gain power and assert his independence against the interests of the organisation as a whole.
Suitability — write this in the paper

A divisional structure suits a business enterprise where a large variety of products is manufactured using different productive resources, and where the organisation has grown so that more employees, more departments and new levels of management have become necessary.

A word students misuse

"Autonomous" does not mean "separate company". A division is autonomous within limits set by the Managing Director. Writing that the divisions are independent firms is wrong.

The same firm, reorganised

Case 6 · Suryodaya Consumer Products Ltd

Suryodaya began in 1998 making only bathing soap, on a functional chart. It now makes soap, detergent powder and shampoo. Soap needs a saponification plant and sells through kirana stores; shampoo needs a blending and bottling line and sells largely through modern trade and e-commerce; the Marketing Manager admits he cannot judge a shampoo launch and a soap price cut in the same week. Profit for 2025-26 fell 12% and nobody could say which product caused it.

Question 5 6 marks

Which structure should Suryodaya adopt? Name it, and give five benefits it will derive from the change. (6 marks → 5–6 points. Name the structure in point 1; that alone is a mark.)

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. It should adopt a divisional structurewith a separate division for soaps, detergents and shampoos, each multifunctional and headed by a divisional manager.
  2. Product specialisationa divisional head who handles only shampoo will learn its plant, its trade and its customer, which no common Marketing Manager can.
  3. Fixation of responsibility for profiteach division becomes a profit centre, so the 12% fall can be traced to a product instead of vanishing into a company total.
  4. Faster decision making and initiativeeach division is autonomous, so a soap price cut need not wait for a shampoo launch to be decided.
  5. Managerial developmentdivisional heads gain all-round experience and become the pool from which top management is later filled.
  6. Easy expansiona fourth product line can be added as a new division without disturbing the existing three.
…the Marketing Manager admits he cannot judge a shampoo launch and a soap price cut in the same week.

Different plants + different channels + one manager who cannot hold both = the textbook argument for divisionalisation, planted in one sentence.

Confusable pair 1 — functional vs divisional structure

What students actually write

"Functional structure is for small companies and divisional structure is for big companies."

"In a divisional structure the work is divided among departments like production and marketing."

"Divisional structure is better than functional structure."

Why each is wrong
  1. Size is not the testboth structures suit a large organisation. The test is the number of product lines, not the number of people.
  2. That sentence describes a functional structureif you divided by production and marketing, you divided by function. A divisional structure divides by product first, and finds the functions inside each division.
  3. Neither is "better"each is better for a different firm. Divisional duplicates resources and costs more; functional is cheaper but cannot fix profit responsibility.

The case that can only go one way

Case 7 · Blue Kite Advertising, Mumbai

Blue Kite has grown to 90 people. It does exactly one thing — advertising campaigns — but the work has clear stages. Every campaign, whichever client it is for, passes through the same copy team, the same art team, the same film production team and the same media buying team; a copywriter writes for a bank one week and a biscuit brand the next. Farhan is deciding between a client-wise structure and a craft-wise one.

Question 6 4 marks

Which structure suits Blue Kite? Name it, justify it from the case, and state one disadvantage Farhan must guard against.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. A functional structuregrouping jobs of a similar nature — copy, art, film, media buying — into separate departments under one head.
  2. Justification — a single product linethe agency sells one thing, campaigns; the clients are customers, not product lines. Grouping by client would be grouping by customer, and would fragment each craft.
  3. Justification — the same specialists serve every joba copywriter works across a bank and a biscuit brand, so the specialised skill, not the account, is the natural unit of grouping.
  4. The disadvantage to guard againstconflict of interest and problems in coordination between craft departments — the art team and the media team optimising their own work while a client's campaign falls between them.

★ Challenge — the structure question in disguise

Case 8 · Suryodaya Consumer Products Ltd

Two years after divisionalising, Anjali finds the Shampoos Division has hired its own three-person payroll clerk team, the Soaps Division has hired two, and the Detergents Division one — six people doing the same work in three rooms. The Shampoos head has also refused to release ₹80 lakh of surplus cash to fund the detergent plant upgrade, saying "that money was earned by my division."

Challenge 4 marks

Anjali's finance head says "this proves divisional structure was a mistake — go back to functional." Do you agree? Identify the two disadvantages showing up, and say what she should actually do.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. Do not agree — these are known costs, not proof of errorthe reason for divisionalising (three product lines, different plants, different channels) has not changed.
  2. Disadvantage 1 — increase in costssix payroll clerks where two would do is exactly the duplication of activities across divisions that this structure carries.
  3. Disadvantage 2 — conflict between divisions and ignoring organisational intereststhe Shampoos head is maximising his own division's position at the cost of another and asserting independence against the firm as a whole.
  4. What she should dokeep the divisions, but centralise the common support services — payroll, legal, IT — and reserve the allocation of funds as a top management decision. Divisional autonomy is autonomy within limits, not sovereignty.
Exam note

"Should we go back?" questions are almost never answered "yes". The board wants you to name the disadvantage, accept it as the price of the structure, and control it.

Formal organisation

Everything so far — the chart, the departments, the reporting lines — is the formal organisation. It is drawn on purpose, by somebody, for a reason.

Definition — exam wording

Formal organisation refers to the organisation structure deliberately designed by the management to accomplish a particular task. It clearly specifies the boundaries of authority and responsibility, and provides systematic coordination among the various activities so that organisational goals are achieved.

Louis Allen: "a system of well-defined jobs, each bearing a definite measure of authority, responsibility and accountability."

Features

  1. Specifies relationshipsit states the nature of the inter-relationship between job positions — who has to report to whom.
  2. A means to achieve the plan's objectivesit lays down the rules and procedures essential for reaching them.
  3. Coordinates departmentsthe efforts of departments are interlinked and integrated through it.
  4. Deliberately designed by top managementit does not appear by itself.
  5. Emphasis on work, not on peopleit places more emphasis on the work to be performed than on the interpersonal relationships among employees.

Formal organisation — advantages and limitations

Advantages

  1. Easier to fix responsibilitymutual relationships are clearly defined.
  2. No ambiguity in rolesduties are specified, so effort is not duplicated.
  3. Unity of command is maintainedthrough an established chain of command.
  4. Effective accomplishment of goalseveryone knows the role he has to play.
  5. Stabilitybehaviour can be fairly predicted, because specific rules guide it.

Limitations

  1. Procedural delaysthe established chain of command must be followed, which slows decision making.
  2. Inadequate recognition of creative talentrigid policies allow no deviation.
  3. An incomplete pictureit emphasises structure and work, so it cannot capture all the human relationships in the enterprise.
Example

At Doon Valley School a teacher who wants a projector must route the request through the Head of Department, the Vice Principal and the Principal. Nothing is lost — but nothing is quick either. That is a procedural delay, and it is the price of the clarity on the other side of the slide.

Informal organisation

Definition — exam wording

Informal organisation is the network of social relationships among employees that emerges spontaneously within the formal organisation when people interact beyond their officially defined roles. It has no written rules, is fluid in form and scope, and has no fixed lines of communication.

Features

  1. Originates within the formal organisationas a result of personal interaction among employees — it cannot exist without a formal organisation to grow inside.
  2. Standards of behaviour come from group normsnot from officially laid down rules and regulations.
  3. Independent channels of communicationwith no specified direction of flow — this informal channel is what Chapter 7 will call the grapevine.
  4. Emerges spontaneouslyit is not deliberately created by management.
  5. No definite structure or formit is a complex network of social relationships, so it cannot be drawn as a chart.
Example

The eleven Blue Kite employees who play cricket at Shivaji Park every Sunday are an informal organisation. Nobody appointed them, no rule created them, and the captain is not the senior-most person in the office.

The two organisations, drawn over each other

Informal organisation — advantages and disadvantages

Advantages

  1. Faster spread of informationprescribed lines of communication are not followed, so information and feedback move quickly.
  2. Fulfils social needsmembers find like-minded people, which raises job satisfaction and gives a sense of belongingness.
  3. Compensates for inadequacies in the formal organisationemployees' reactions to a plan or policy can be tested through the informal network before it is announced.

Disadvantages

  1. Spreads rumoursit becomes a destructive force working against the interest of the formal organisation.
  2. Resistance to changeif the informal organisation opposes a change, management may fail to implement it, delaying or restricting growth.
  3. Pressure to conformmembers are pressed to accept group expectations, which is harmful when the group's norms are against organisational interests.
The examiner's favourite line

Informal organisation cannot be eliminated. Management should recognise it, identify the roles its members play, and use it as a communication channel and a source of support — not confront it.

Confusable pair 2 — formal vs informal organisation

What students actually write

"Informal organisation is the organisation where there are no rules, like a small shop."

"Informal organisation is created by the management for social activities of employees."

"Formal organisation is good and informal organisation is bad and should be removed."

Why each is wrong
  1. It is not a kind of firminformal organisation is not a small or casual company — it is a network of social relationships that exists inside a formal organisation, including inside the strictest one.
  2. Management does not create itit emerges spontaneously. A staff club started by the HR department is part of the formal organisation, because management deliberately designed it.
  3. It cannot be removed, and it is usefulthe exam answer is that management should recognise and use it. Only its misuse — rumour, resistance — is a disadvantage.

A case that resolves only one way

Case 9 · Suryodaya Consumer Products Ltd

Anjali circulated a note on 3 August 2026 proposing a rotating night shift at the Nashik detergent plant. Delegation

The chart now exists. But a manager who keeps every decision on his own desk has a chart and no organisation.

Definition — exam wording

Delegation refers to the downward transfer of authority from a superior to a subordinate. Louis Allen: delegation is the entrustment of responsibility and authority to another, and the creation of accountability for performance.

The sentence that carries a mark on its own

Delegation does not mean abdication. The manager who delegates remains accountable for the performance of the assigned task, to exactly the same extent as before. He may also take the authority back and re-delegate it to somebody else.

Example

Hasmukh Vora gave Ramesh Solanki the right to stop a kiln batch without asking anyone. If a batch is spoiled, Ramesh answers to Hasmukh — and Hasmukh still answers to the board.

The three elements of delegation

Watch the drawing build. Each press adds one element — and the third is the one students lose marks on.

Element 1 — authority

Definition — exam wording

Authority refers to the right of an individual to command his subordinates and to take action within the scope of his position. It arises from the established scalar chain, flows downward from superior to subordinate, and can be delegated.

  1. It arises from position, not from the personin the formal organisation authority originates by virtue of the post held; it is highest at the top and reduces as you go down the ladder.
  2. It is limitedby law and by the rules and regulations of the organisation. Authority is never unlimited, even at the top.
  3. It maintains orderit gives the manager the right to exact obedience and give directions to the workforce under him.
Case 11 · Kathiawar Ceramics, Rajkot

Ramesh Solanki, the Kiln Supervisor, may stop a firing batch and requisition up to ₹25,000 of glaze from stores without asking anyone. Last month he refused a moulder's request for two days' casual leave and the moulder took it anyway. Ramesh was told that leave is sanctioned by the HR Manager, not by a production supervisor, whatever the post-holder's seniority.

Question 8 3 marks

Ramesh says "I am a supervisor, so I should be able to decide leave." Which two characteristics of authority is he ignoring? Name and explain each, and quote the line that settles it.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. Authority arises from the position, not the personhis right to command comes from the post of Kiln Supervisor, and that post carries production decisions only — his seniority adds nothing to it.
  2. Authority is always limitedit is bounded by the rules and regulations of the organisation. Ramesh's limits are ₹25,000 and the production function; leave sanction sits inside another post's boundary altogether.
  3. Why this matters for delegationa superior can delegate only the authority he himself holds, and only within those limits — which is why the next two elements, responsibility and accountability, have to be matched to it exactly.
Ramesh was told that leave is sanctioned by the HR Manager, not by a production supervisor, whatever the post-holder's seniority.

"Whatever the post-holder's seniority" is the examiner telling you the answer is position, not person. Underline that clause in the question paper before you start writing.

Element 2 — responsibility

Definition — exam wording

Responsibility is the obligation of a subordinate to properly perform the assigned duty. It arises from a superior–subordinate relationship, flows upward, and cannot be entirely delegated.

The rule the paper tests every year

Authority granted must be commensurate with the responsibility assigned.

  1. If authority is more than responsibilityit leads to the misuse of authority.
  2. If responsibility is more than authorityit makes the person ineffective — he is answerable for a result he was never given the means to produce.
Case 12 · Kathiawar Ceramics, Rajkot

The Works Manager asked the foreman to raise output to 200 sanitaryware pieces a day. He did not give him the right to requisition tools or materials from the stores department; every indent still needs the Works Manager's own signature. Output stayed at 150.

Question 9 3 marks

Can the Works Manager blame the foreman? Give reasons.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. No, he cannotthe foreman was assigned responsibility for 200 pieces a day but was not delegated the authority to obtain tools and materials.
  2. The principle violatedauthority must be commensurate with responsibility — for effective delegation the two must match.
  3. The consequence named in the syllabuswhere responsibility exceeds authority, the person is rendered ineffective; the fault lies with the delegating manager.
He did not give him the right to requisition tools or materials from the stores department; every indent still needs the Works Manager's own signature.

Element 3 — accountability

Definition — exam wording

Accountability implies being answerable for the final outcome of the assigned task. It arises from responsibility, flows upward, cannot be delegated at all, and is generally enforced through regular feedback.

Learn this sentence exactly — it separates all three

Authority is delegated, responsibility is assumed, accountability is imposed.

And the chain of origin: responsibility is derived from authority; accountability is derived from responsibility.

Case 13 · Blue Kite Advertising, Mumbai

Farhan delegated the entire Ganesh Chaturthi campaign for a paint client to Nikhil, with authority to approve artwork and book media up to ₹40 lakh. The campaign ran a wrong product code in every newspaper insert. The client called Farhan. Farhan replied, "I had handed the whole account to Nikhil — this is his failure, not the agency's."

Question 10 4 marks

Is Farhan's defence valid? Name the element of delegation involved, explain it, and state the two errors in his sentence.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. The defence is not validthe element involved is accountability.
  2. Accountability cannot be delegated at allit is answerability for the final outcome; Nikhil is accountable to Farhan, and Farhan remains accountable to the client to exactly the same extent as before he delegated.
  3. Error 1 — delegation is not abdicationa manager may hand over authority; he cannot hand over the answer he owes his own superior.
  4. Error 2 — accountability should have been enforcedit is enforced through regular feedback; had Farhan reviewed the inserts before release, the outcome would have been caught.
Farhan replied, "I had handed the whole account to Nikhil — this is his failure, not the agency's."
"Cannot be entirely delegated" vs "cannot be delegated at all"

The two phrases are not sloppy repetition — the difference is examined.

Responsibility cannot be entirely delegated. A manager can pass a part of his duty down: Farhan really did make Nikhil responsible for the artwork. What he cannot do is empty his own obligation — some responsibility for the task always stays with him. So the transfer is partial.

Accountability cannot be delegated at all. Answerability for the final outcome does not divide. Nikhil becomes accountable to Farhan; that does not reduce Farhan's accountability to the client by a rupee. So the transfer is zero.

In one line for the paper: a manager may share the work, but never the answer. Authority — delegated. Responsibility — partly. Accountability — never.

Confusable pair 3 — authority, responsibility and accountability

What students actually write

"Authority and responsibility are the same thing — the right and duty to do the work."

"If a manager delegates authority, his responsibility also goes to the subordinate."

"Accountability is another word for responsibility."

Why each is wrong
  1. They travel in opposite directionsauthority flows down, responsibility flows up. Two things moving the other way cannot be the same thing.
  2. Delegating authority does not export responsibilityresponsibility cannot be entirely delegated. The subordinate acquires a new responsibility; the superior does not lose his.
  3. Accountability is the outcome, responsibility is the taskresponsibility is the obligation to perform; accountability is answerability for the result of performing it, and it is the only one of the three that cannot be delegated at all.

Importance of delegation

  1. Effective managementthe manager is freed from routine work and gets time to concentrate on important matters and excel in new areas.
  2. Employee developmentsubordinates get the chance to use their skills, gain experience and develop for higher positions — it produces better future managers.
  3. Motivation of employeesentrusting a task involves trust on the superior's part and commitment on the subordinate's; responsibility builds self-esteem.
  4. Facilitation of growthit produces a ready workforce of trained people to take up leading positions in new ventures and branches.
  5. Basis of management hierarchydelegation establishes superior–subordinate relationships; it is the degree and flow of authority that determines who reports to whom.
  6. Better coordinationthe three elements define powers, duties and answerability at every post, so duties do not overlap and effort is not duplicated.
Example

Centralisation and decentralisation

Definitions — exam wording

Decentralisation refers to the systematic delegation of decision-making authority throughout all the levels of the organisation, so that authority is shared with lower levels and placed nearest to the points of action. It is an optional policy decision of top management.

Centralisation is the opposite: decision-making authority is retained by the higher levels of management.

They are relative terms — never absolute

The sentence the paper wants

No organisation is ever completely centralised or completely decentralised.

  1. Complete centralisationwould concentrate every decision at the apex — which would remove the need for a management hierarchy altogether.
  2. Complete decentralisationwould push every decision to the lowest level — which would remove the need for higher management altogether.
  3. So every organisation has bothand the real question is only the degree, which shifts towards decentralisation as a firm grows in size and complexity.
The word appears in two chapters — do not mix them

In Chapter 2, centralisation and decentralisation is one of Fayol's fourteen principles, and the principle is about keeping a balance between them. In Chapter 5 decentralisation is a policy — the systematic delegation of authority to all levels. Same words, different question. Read which chapter the question is set in.

Importance of decentralisation

  1. Develops initiative among subordinatesfreedom to decide teaches lower levels to rely on their own judgement, and identifies who has the potential to lead.
  2. Develops managerial talent for the futurehandling assignments independently creates a reservoir of qualified manpower for promotion — training alone cannot do this.
  3. Quick decision makingdecisions are taken nearest the point of action and need no approval from many levels, so information is neither slow nor distorted.
  4. Relief to top managementdirect supervision is replaced by other forms of control, leaving top management free for policy decisions rather than operational ones.
  5. Facilitates growthgreater autonomy lets each department work in the way that suits it and fosters healthy competition, raising productivity and returns for expansion.
  6. Better controlperformance can be evaluated at each level and each department held accountable for its own results — through systems such as the balanced scorecard and management information systems.
Caution — the balancing sentence

Decentralisation can lead to organisational disintegration if departments start working to their own guidelines. It must always be balanced with centralisation in areas of major policy.

Confusable pair 4 — delegation vs decentralisation

What students actually write

"Decentralisation is delegation on a larger scale — they are the same thing."

"Both are compulsory, because a manager cannot do all the work himself."

"A manager who gives authority to his assistant has decentralised the organisation."

Why each is wrong
  1. Scale is a symptom, not the definitiondecentralisation is delegation extended to the lowest level as a policy. The difference is not "more of it" but who decided, and how far it reaches.
  2. Only one of them is compulsorydelegation is compulsory — no individual can perform every task. Decentralisation is optional, a deliberate policy decision of top management, and a firm may choose to stay centralised.
  3. One superior and one subordinate is delegationa single transfer of authority creates a superior–subordinate relationship. Decentralisation requires the transfer to run throughout all levels of the organisation.

Distinguish — delegation and decentralisation

Five bases shown; use any four. "Nature" and "Scope" are the two that decide most case questions.
BasisDelegationDecentralisation

ScopeNarrow — limited to a superior and his immediate subordinate.Wide — delegation extended to the lowest level of management.

One-line link

Every decentralisation contains delegation; not every delegation amounts to decentralisation.

  2. Benefit — faster communication and quick feedbackinformation and grievances will move without waiting on the scalar chain.
  3. Benefit — fulfilment of social needsa sense of belongingness raises job satisfaction, which is the direct remedy for high employee turnover.
  4. Benefit — it compensates for the formal organisation's inadequaciesthe creative suggestions the formal system rejects will surface, and reactions to policy can be tested before announcement.

The examiner does not want "abolish the formal organisation". The formal structure stays; the informal one is recognised and used.

Decisions are delayed because every proposal must climb the full chain of command, workers can raise a grievance only through written formal channels involving long procedures, and creative suggestions from the shop floor are rejected because they deviate from laid-down policy.

One sentence, three marks. Each clause is one named limitation — delay, no route for grievances, no recognition of creative talent. Split a long case sentence at its commas before you decide how many points to write.

Before you leave this chapter

The four traps, in the order they cost marks
  1. Writing the organising process when the question asked for structureread the noun in the question.
  2. Calling a one-step transfer "decentralisation"ask whether it reached the lowest level, and whether it was a policy.
  3. Saying responsibility moves to the subordinateresponsibility cannot be entirely delegated; accountability cannot be delegated at all.
  4. Two paragraphs where a "distinguish" table was asked forno basis column, no marks — however right the content is.
