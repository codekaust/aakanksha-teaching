Chapter 7
Directing

A plan, a structure and a staff are three kinds of preparation. Nothing moves until someone instructs, guides, motivates and leads. Directing is the function that initiates action — and it is the only function of management performed on people rather than on work.

Where we were

Chapters 4, 5 and 6 → Chapter 7

Chapter 4 produced a plan. Chapter 5 turned it into a structure — departments, posts, authority relationships. Chapter 6 put people into those posts, trained them and appraised them.

And still nothing has happened. A plan on paper, a chart on a wall and thirty trained employees at their desks produce no output at all. Somebody has to say start, and keep saying it in a way that people willingly follow. That is directing.

The line worth remembering

Planning, organising and staffing prepare a setting for action. Directing initiates action. Controlling then checks whether the action went where the plan said — which is Chapter 8.

The route through this chapter

Directing is one function with four elements. Three of the four are large enough to be examined on their own.

Weight in the paper

Units 6, 7 and 8 together carry 20 marks of the 80 — the largest block in the paper, and Directing is the largest chapter inside it. Motivation and Communication are the two sections the board returns to year after year, almost always as a case.

What directing means

Definition — learn this wording

Directing refers to the process of instructing, guiding, counselling, motivating and leading people in the organisation to achieve its objectives.

Five verbs, and the examiner is scanning for them. "Directing means telling workers what to do" is worth almost nothing — it drops counselling, motivating and leading, which are the three that make directing more than order-giving.

Case 1 · Deccan Autocomp, Chakan, Pune

Sanjay Kulkarni, plant head of a 400-worker auto-components unit, has a production plan, a three-shift structure and a fully staffed press shop. At 6 a.m. on Monday he walks the floor. He explains the new brake-disc tolerance to each shift in-charge, sits ten minutes with a demoralised operator whose machine failed twice last week, and announces a shift-wise output board so the best line is named at the gate.

Read the case against the definition

Explaining the tolerance is instructing and guiding. Ten minutes with the demoralised operator is counselling. The output board is motivating. Walking the floor at 6 a.m. himself is leading. One three-line paragraph, four of the five verbs.

Features of directing

Four features. Each one is a one-line heading plus one line of explanation — that is exactly the form the answer takes.

1 · Directing initiates action

The other four functions prepare a setting for action. Directing is where the organisation actually begins to work.

2 · It takes place at every level

Every manager from the managing director to the shop-floor supervisor directs. Directing exists wherever a superior–subordinate relationship exists.

3 · It is a continuous process

It runs throughout the life of the organisation. Managers change; the directing does not stop.

4 · It flows from top to bottom

Directing is initiated at the top and flows down the hierarchy. Every manager directs his immediate subordinate and takes instructions from his immediate boss.

The feature students state wrongly

"Directing takes place at every level" and "directing flows from top to bottom" are two different features, not one. The first says who does it — everybody. The second says which way it travels — downward, one link at a time along the scalar chain. Fusing them costs a mark.

How directing travels through the organisation

How to read this
  1. Start at the top box and read downwards — that is the direction of flow.
  2. Each solid amber arrow is one superior directing his immediate subordinate.
  3. The dashed cyan arrow runs upward: that is feedback, not directing.
  4. Every middle box has an arrow in and an arrow out — that is "every level directs".

Why directing matters

Five points. Points = marks in this subject: a 5-mark question wants five headed points, not five paragraphs and not three.

  1. Initiates actionpeople begin to work towards the objective only when someone instructs and guides them.
  2. Integrates employees' effortsindividual efforts are welded together so that each one contributes to organisational performance, not to a private target.
  3. Helps employees realise their potentialthrough motivation and effective leadership a manager extracts work up to a person's full capability.
  4. Facilitates the introduction of changepeople resist change; good directing reduces the resistance by explaining, training and rewarding.
  5. Brings stability and balanceit fosters cooperation and commitment and balances the interests of different groups and departments.
Example for point 4 — dated

When Hooghly Jute Mills, Kolkata, moved its ledgers to a computerised accounting package in 2024, the accounts staff resisted. The manager explained the purpose, ran a two-week training batch and offered a completion allowance. Resistance fell — directing facilitated the change.

Question — importance of directing

Question 1 4 marks

Hotel Amber Palace, Jaipur, is introducing a new digital check-in system. The front-office staff are anxious that it will cost them their jobs and are quietly refusing to use it. The general manager holds a briefing, arranges training and announces that no post will be cut. Explain, with reference to the case, any four points of importance of the directing function.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer — four marks, four headed points
  1. Directing facilitates the introduction of changethe briefing and the assurance on jobs reduced the staff's resistance to the new system.
  2. Directing initiates actionthe system existed but was unused; the manager's instructions are what set it in motion.
  3. Directing integrates employees' effortsfront office, housekeeping and billing must now work off one system towards the same guest-service goal.
  4. Directing brings stability and balancethe assurance on jobs restored the cooperation and commitment the department needed to function.

A fifth point — helping employees realise their potential — would earn nothing extra. Four marks, four points, then stop.

The four elements of directing

How to read this
  1. The amber box is the function; the four blue boxes are its elements, not its steps.
  2. They are not sequential — a manager uses all four in the same conversation.
  3. In a case question, first ask which of these four is being described.

Principles of directing

Eight guiding principles. Group them, then learn them — the paper usually asks for any three.

Hook Maximum contribution · Harmony of objectives · Unity of command · Appropriate technique · Communication · Informal organisation · Leadership · Follow through

Maximum individual contribution

Directing techniques must help every individual contribute to his maximum potential — a good incentive plan is the usual example.

Harmony of objectives

Individual goals (salary) and organisational goals (productivity) conflict. Directing must convince people the two are complementary.

Unity of command

A person should receive instructions from one superior only; two bosses create confusion, conflict and disorder.

Appropriateness of direction technique

Match the motivational and leadership technique to the subordinate's needs and abilities — money moves one person, promotion another.

Managerial communication

Instructions must create total understanding, and feedback must confirm that the subordinate understood them.

Use of informal organisation

Informal groups exist inside every formal structure. A manager should spot them and use them rather than fight them.

Leadership

Direct through good leadership, which influences subordinates positively without causing dissatisfaction.

Follow through

Giving an order is not enough. Review whether it is being implemented and modify the direction if it is not.

Supervision — the first element

Definition — learn this wording

Supervision means overseeing what is being done by subordinates and giving them instructions to ensure the optimum utilisation of resources and the achievement of work targets.

The word has two senses — and the paper uses both

As an element of directing — supervision is performed by every manager at every level. The managing director supervises his general managers.

As a level of management — the supervisor is a managerial position at the operative level, immediately above the worker. He is the link between workers and management.

Case 3 · Hooghly Jute Mills, Kolkata

Debashis Sen is the weaving-shed supervisor over 46 loom operators. He checks the yarn tension on every loom twice a shift, shows a new operator how to re-thread a broken warp, and carries the operators' complaint about the leaking shed roof to the mill manager the same evening.

Both senses in one man

Checking the looms is supervision as an element of directing. Carrying the complaint upward is what only the supervisory level can do, because he is the only manager in daily physical contact with the workers.

Confusable pair — supervision as an element vs the supervisor as a level

Students routinely write…

"Supervision is done by the supervisory level of management, which is the lowest level." — and then lose the marks in a question about the elements of directing, where the required point is the exact opposite: that supervision is performed by every manager at every level.

Three bases is the right length for a 3-mark "distinguish" answer.
BasisSupervision as an element of directingSupervisor as a level of management
Meaning The process of overseeing subordinates' work and instructing them. A managerial position in the hierarchy, placed immediately above the workers.
Who performs it Every manager at every level, wherever a superior–subordinate relation exists. Only the person holding that post — foreman, shift in-charge, section officer.
Scope One of the four elements of directing; part of a function. The operative / lowest level of the three levels of management.
Special role Ensures optimum use of resources and achievement of targets. Acts as the link between workers and management — no other manager touches the workers daily.

The importance of supervision — the supervisor's seven roles

  1. Guide, friend and philosopherhe maintains day-to-day contact and friendly relations with workers.
  2. Link between workers and managementhe carries management's ideas down and the workers' problems up, avoiding misunderstanding and conflict.
  3. Maintains group unityhe sorts out internal differences and keeps harmony among the workers under him.
  4. Ensures performance against targetshe takes responsibility for task achievement and motivates his workers to reach it.
  5. Provides on-the-job traininga skilled supervisor builds an efficient team at the bench itself.
  6. Provides supervisory leadershipa supervisor with leadership qualities builds high morale among workers.
  7. Analyses work and gives feedbackhe tells the worker how he performed and suggests ways of developing his skill.
Sizing the answer

This list is the answer to a 5- or 6-mark question. For 3 marks pick the three that fit the case in front of you — almost always link between workers and management, on-the-job training and feedback.

Question 2 3 marks

At Hooghly Jute Mills the loom operators refused a change in shift timings. Debashis Sen explained to them why the export order needed a night shift, and on the same day told the mill manager that the workers wanted the canteen kept open till midnight. Both sides agreed. Answer — 3 marks, 3 points

Motivation — the second element

Definition — learn this wording

Motivation is the process of stimulating people to action to accomplish desired goals. It depends upon satisfying the needs of people.

Three words arrive together and are constantly swapped in answers. Keep them apart.

Motive — inside the person

An inner state that energises, activates or moves and directs behaviour towards goals. Motives arise out of needs — hunger, security, affiliation, recognition.

Motivation — the process

The process of stimulating people to action by satisfying those needs.

Incentive / motivator — offered by the firm

All measures used to motivate people to improve performance — pay, bonus, promotion, recognition, responsibility.

The sentence that fixes all three

A worker's motive is his own; motivation is what the manager does about it; the incentive is the thing the manager offers. An incentive works only when it matches the motive — a bonus does nothing for a man whose real need is respect.

The motivation process

How to read this
  1. Start at the rose box, top left, and follow the numbered amber arrows.
  2. The row below runs right to left — follow the arrowheads, not the reading order.
  3. The dashed cyan arrow returns to box 1: a satisfied need stops motivating and a new one takes over.
  4. Rose = the deficiency, green = the resolution.

Features of motivation

1 · Motivation is an internal feeling

The urges, drives, desires and needs that shape behaviour are internal to the individual. A manager cannot install them; he can only satisfy them.

2 · Motivation produces goal-directed behaviour

Promotion offered to improve performance produces exactly that behaviour — if the employee wants the promotion.

3 · Motivation can be positive or negative

Positive — a pay rise, promotion, recognition. Negative — punishment, stopping an increment, a threat. Both can produce the desired action.

4 · Motivation is a complex process

Individuals differ in expectations, perceptions and reactions, so no single incentive works uniformly on all members.

Case 4 · Deccan Autocomp, Pune

Sanjay Kulkarni offered the same ₹8,000 festival bonus to every press-shop operator. Twenty-two operators raised their output; four did not move at all, and one of them told the supervisor that what he actually wanted was to be sent for the die-setting course at ARAI.

Which feature is this?

Motivation is a complex process. The same incentive produced three different responses because the underlying motives differed — and the fourth man's motive was self-development, which money does not touch.

The importance of motivation

  1. Improves performance levelssatisfied employees devote their full energy to the work, so both individual and organisational performance rise.
  2. Changes negative or indifferent attitudes into positive onessuitable rewards and praise turn an unwilling worker into a willing one.
  3. Reduces employee turnovermotivated employees do not leave, which saves the cost of fresh recruitment and training, and retains talent.
  4. Reduces absenteeismbad conditions, poor rewards and lack of recognition cause absence; a sound motivational system removes them and work becomes a source of pleasure.
  5. Helps introduce changes smoothlyif employees are convinced the change brings additional rewards, resistance falls away.
Watch the wording

"Reduces employee turnover" and "reduces absenteeism" are separate points. Turnover is people leaving; absenteeism is people not turning up. Merging them into "employees stay" collapses two marks into one.

Maslow's Need Hierarchy Theory

Definition — learn this wording

Abraham Maslow (1943) held that within every human being there exists a hierarchy of five needs: basic physiological → safety and security → affiliation/belonging → esteem → self-actualisation.

The theory rests on four assumptions, and the assumptions are examinable in their own right.

  1. Behaviour is based on needspeople's behaviour is influenced by the satisfaction of their needs.
  2. Needs are in a hierarchical orderthey run from the basic needs upward to the higher-level needs.
  3. A satisfied need no longer motivatesonly the next higher need can motivate a person once the lower one is met.
  4. Movement is upward and conditionala person moves to the next level only when the lower need is satisfied.
The order is the answer

Write the five needs in the wrong order and the answer is wrong even if all five names are present. Physiological, safety and security, affiliation/belonging, esteem, self-actualisation. Never "social needs" alone — write affiliation/belonging.

The hierarchy, with a real incentive at every level

Applying Maslow to a case

Case 5 · Hooghly Jute Mills, Kolkata

The mill pays above the statutory minimum wage and runs a subsidised canteen; nobody complains about money. But after two contract weavers were let go last March, the permanent operators began refusing overtime and asked the union for a written assurance that no permanent post would be cut. A ₹3,000 productivity bonus announced in April changed nothing.

Question 4 4 marks

Identify the level of need in Maslow's hierarchy that is operating here, explain why the bonus failed, and name two incentives the mill should have offered instead.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. The need is safety and securitythese needs provide security and protection from physical and emotional harm — job security and stability of income.
  2. Why the bonus failedphysiological needs were already satisfied by the wage and the canteen, and a satisfied need no longer motivates. More money aimed at a need nobody had.
  3. Incentive one — job securitya written assurance of permanence, so that employees have certainty about future income and work.
  4. Incentive two — retirement benefitsprovident fund, pension and gratuity, which give financial security beyond the present month.
"…the permanent operators began refusing overtime and asked the union for a written assurance that no permanent post would be cut."

★ Challenge — the two levels students always swap

Case 6 · Hotel Amber Palace, Jaipur

Nandini Rathore, the general manager, does two things in the same week. She converts eighteen seasonal banquet staff into permanent employees with provident fund, and she creates the post of "Guest Relations Lead" with a name badge and a reserved parking slot for the best-rated attendant.

Challenge 4 marks

Answer
  1. Permanency and provident fund → safety and security needsthey give protection and certainty about future income and work, not admiration.
  2. The new post, badge and parking slot → esteem needsthese are status, recognition and attention — self-respect in the eyes of others.

Financial incentives — the seven

Definition — learn this wording

Financial incentives are incentives which are in direct monetary form or measurable in monetary terms and serve to satisfy the economic needs of employees and motivate them to better performance. They may be given on an individual or a group basis.

1 · Pay and allowances

Basic pay, dearness allowance and other allowances, with annual increments. In some firms the hike is linked to performance.

2 · Productivity-linked wage incentives

Wage plans that link payment directly to an increase in productivity, individually or by group.

3 · Bonus

An amount paid over and above the wage or salary.

4 · Profit sharing

Giving employees a share in the profits of the organisation, so that they work to raise those profits.

5 · Co-partnership / stock option

Company shares offered at a price below the market price. Share allotment creates a feeling of ownership.

6 · Retirement benefits

Provident fund, pension and gratuity — financial security after retirement, which motivates during service.

7 · Perquisites

Fringe benefits over and above salary — car allowance, housing, medical aid, children's education.

The examiner's favourite trap

Ask who bears the risk. Bonus is paid whether or not profits rise; profit sharing is paid only out of profit; a stock option makes the employee a part-owner.

Non-financial incentives — the eight

Definition — learn this wording

Non-financial incentives focus on the psychological, social and emotional needs of employees rather than on money. Money may sometimes be involved, but the emphasis is on psychological and emotional satisfaction.

1 · Status

The ranking of a position — the authority, responsibility, rewards, recognition, perquisites and prestige attached to it. Satisfies psychological, social and esteem needs.

2 · Organisational climate

The characteristics that distinguish one organisation from another and influence behaviour — individual autonomy, reward orientation, consideration to employees, risk-taking.

3 · Career advancement opportunity

Skill-development programmes plus a sound promotion policy. "Promotion works as a tonic."

4 · Job enrichment

Designing jobs with greater variety of work content, a higher level of skill, more autonomy and responsibility, so that the job itself motivates.

5 · Employee recognition programmes

Acknowledgment with a show of appreciation — congratulating, the notice board, a certificate, a memento, a reward for a valuable suggestion.

6 · Job security

Certainty about future income and work. Its negative side: employees who cannot lose their jobs may become complacent.

7 · Employee participation

Involving employees in decisions that concern them — joint management committees, work committees, canteen committees.

8 · Employee empowerment

Giving more autonomy and power to subordinates, so that they feel their jobs are important.

Confusable pair — financial vs non-financial incentives

Students routinely write…

"Non-financial incentives are used when the company has no money to spend, because they cost nothing." — Both halves are wrong. Job enrichment needs redesigned jobs and fresh training; a recognition programme needs certificates, an event and a manager's time; career advancement needs a whole skill-development budget; a promotion almost always carries more pay.

The classification does not depend on whether money is spent. It depends on which need is being satisfied — economic, or psychological/social/emotional.

Four bases. Use three of them for a 3-mark question and all four for 4 marks.

Leadership — the third element

Definition — learn this wording

Leadership is the process of influencing the behaviour of people by making them strive voluntarily towards the achievement of organisational goals.

Koontz's wording — "so that they strive willingly and enthusiastically towards the achievement of group goals" — is equally acceptable. Either way the two load-bearing words are influencing and voluntarily / willingly: influence that has to be enforced is authority, not leadership.

Five features. Notice that three of them are about the followers, not about the leader.

  1. Ability to influence othersleadership is shown by the capacity to make others act, not by the position held.
  2. Tries to bring about a change in behaviourinfluence that leaves behaviour unchanged is not leadership.
  3. Indicates interpersonal relationsit exists only in the relation between a leader and his followers.
  4. Exercised to achieve common goalsthe goals must be the group's, not the leader's private ones.
  5. A continuous processit is exercised day after day, not on one occasion.
Followers make the leader

The success of an organisation is credited to its leader, but the followers' skills, commitment, willingness to cooperate and team spirit are what make a person an effective leader. Leadership requires the existence of followers — a manager whom nobody follows is exercising authority, not leadership.

The importance of leadership

  1. Influences behaviour towards the organisation's benefita good leader produces good results through his followers.
  2. Maintains personal relations and helps fulfil needshe provides confidence, support and encouragement, creating a congenial work environment.
  3. Introduces required changeshe persuades, clarifies and inspires, so change is accepted with minimum discontentment.
  4. Handles conflicts effectivelyhe lets followers ventilate their disagreement, then persuades them with clarifications.
  5. Trains and builds a successora good leader develops subordinates so that succession is smooth.
Case 10 · Hooghly Jute Mills, Kolkata

When the mill switched from a two-shift to a three-shift pattern, the weavers threatened a stoppage. The mill manager sat through a four-hour meeting letting every objection be spoken, then explained the export order line by line and worked the first night shift himself. The stoppage was called off and the shift ran.

Which two points is this?

Handles conflicts effectively — he let the followers ventilate their disagreement and then persuaded them. Introduces required changes — the new shift went in with minimum discontentment. Working the first night shift himself is leadership by example.

The three leadership styles — a continuum of authority

Autocratic / authoritarian leadership

Definition — learn this wording

An autocratic leader gives orders and expects his subordinates to obey them. All authority is centralised in the leader, communication is one-way, and he relies on reward and punishment. He is dogmatic — he does not wish to be contradicted.

When it works

Where quick decisions are essential, where subordinates are unskilled or need close control, and where the manager alone is answerable for output on time — a factory floor is the standard example.

What it costs

Employees take no initiative, fear expressing opinions, and work under stress. Their esteem and self-actualisation needs go unmet.

Case 11 · Deccan Autocomp, Pune

The maintenance head, Mr Deshpande, decides the annual shutdown plan alone. When a junior engineer suggested resequencing two jobs, he was told that the plan was final and that his business was to execute it. Engineers have stopped bringing suggestions; two resigned in 2025.

The exact phrase the paper uses

The board's own wording for this case is: "the problem is in the way authority is being used" — the manager is using an autocratic style, which suppresses initiative.

Democratic / participative leadership

Definition — learn this wording

A democratic leader develops action plans and makes decisions in consultation with his subordinates and encourages them to participate in decision making. Communication is two-way, and he exercises control by using the forces within the group.

Case 12 · Hotel Amber Palace, Jaipur

Nandini Rathore must cut the food-and-beverage cost by 8%. She puts the figure before the executive chef, the purchase officer and two banquet stewards, takes their three proposals, and adopts the one they collectively back — then signs the order herself. The cost falls 9% and no one complains about portion sizes.

The half-mark students throw away

Democratic does not mean the group decides. The leader consults, then decides. A case in which the group votes and the manager merely implements the vote is drifting towards laissez faire — and "she signs the order herself" is the phrase that keeps this one democratic.

Laissez faire / free-rein leadership

Definition — learn this wording

A laissez faire or free-rein leader does not believe in the use of power unless it is absolutely essential. Followers are given a high degree of independence to formulate their own objectives and the ways to achieve them; the manager only supports them and supplies the required information, while the subordinate assumes responsibility for the work.

Case 13 · Vaayu Systems, Chandigarh

The four-person research cell was told to "find something worth patenting by March". They chose the problem, fixed their own milestones and picked the toolchain; the CTO's only interventions were to buy two GPU servers and to answer questions when asked. One patent application was filed in February 2026.

Free-rein is not "no leadership"

Even a laissez faire leader lays down certain rules to be followed, and a democratic leader may have to decide alone in an emergency. A leader chooses a combination of styles according to the situation — write that line whenever a question asks which style is "best".

Distinguishing the three styles

Four bases across three columns. In the exam, name the basis in the first column — a table without a basis column loses most of the marks even when the content is right.
BasisAutocraticDemocraticLaissez faire
Authority Fully centralised in the leader. Shared — decisions taken in consultation. Given to the group; the leader keeps almost none.
Communication One-way, downward only. Two-way, upward and downward. Mostly lateral, within the group.
Decision making The leader decides alone. The leader decides after consulting. The subordinates decide and assume responsibility.
Suitability Unskilled staff, emergencies, quick decisions. Experienced and competent subordinates. Highly qualified, self-motivated professionals.

Confusable pair — motivation vs leadership

Students routinely write…

"Motivation and leadership are the same thing, because both make people work." — They are two separate elements of directing, and a case question will punish the fusion. Motivating is one of the things a leader does; it is not the whole of leadership, and a person with no followers can still be motivated.

Three bases is enough for 3 marks; add "basis of influence" for 4.
BasisMotivationLeadership
Meaning The process of stimulating people to action by satisfying their needs. The process of influencing behaviour so that people strive willingly towards group goals.
Basis of influence The need of the individual — pay, security, recognition, growth. The personal influence of the leader and his relationship with followers.
Requires An incentive matched to a motive. No followers are needed. Followers. Without them there is no leadership.
Relationship A tool the leader uses. The wider activity that uses motivation, supervision and communication together.
The case that can only be read one way

"Sanjay Kulkarni raised the night-shift allowance from ₹200 to ₹350 and the number of volunteers doubled." — Motivation: a need was satisfied by an incentive, and no personal influence was exercised. Change it to "Sanjay worked the night shift himself for a week and the volunteers doubled" and it becomes leadership.

Communication — the fourth element

Definition — learn this wording

Communication is the process of exchange of ideas, views, facts and feelings between or among people to create a common understanding.

The word comes from the Latin communis — "common". The whole point of the definition is the phrase common understanding: a message that was sent but not understood is not communication. Write ideas, views, facts and feelings, not just "information" — communication is not only the transfer of data.

Why it matters — 1 to 4

  1. Basis of coordinationit explains the goals, the way to reach them and how people's work interrelates.
  2. Smooth working of the enterprisewhen communication stops, organised activity ceases to exist.
  3. Basis of decision makingno information, no meaningful decision.
  4. Increases managerial efficiencytargets, instructions and allocation of jobs all travel by communication.

Why it matters — 5 to 7

  1. Promotes cooperation and industrial peacetwo-way communication builds mutual understanding between management and workers.
  2. Establishes effective leadershipa leader influences people through communication; poor communication, no influence.
  3. Boosts morale and provides motivationit enables management to motivate, influence and satisfy subordinates.
A figure worth quoting

A manager is estimated to spend about 90% of his time communicating — reading, writing, listening, guiding, instructing. The figure is illustrative, not examinable; the point it makes is not.

The eight elements of the communication process

Sender

The person who conveys his thoughts or ideas to the receiver — the source of the communication.

Message

The content of the idea, feeling, suggestion or order intended to be communicated.

Encoding

Converting the message into communication symbols — words, pictures, gestures.

Media / channel

The path through which the encoded message is transmitted — written, face to face, a phone call, e-mail.

Decoding

Converting the encoded symbols back into meaning by the receiver.

Receiver

The person who receives the communication of the sender.

Noise

Any obstruction or hindrance to communication — a bad phone line, an inattentive receiver, an ambiguous symbol, faulty decoding, a prejudice, a distorting gesture.

Feedback

All those actions of the receiver indicating that he has received and understood the sender's message.

Six or eight?

If the question says "state the elements of the communication process", write all eight. Noise and feedback are elements, not extras — and they are the two that a three-mark case is almost always built on.

The communication process — built up, not listed

How to read this
  1. Read the amber chain left to right — that is the message travelling.
  2. The rose burst is noise: it can strike the encoding, the channel or the decoding, which is why three arrows leave it.
  3. The violet dashed loop runs backwards, from receiver to sender — that is feedback, and it is what closes the process.
  4. Without the loop the sender never learns whether he was understood.
Now name what appeared first — noise

Noise is any obstruction or hindrance to communication, and it may be caused to the sender, the message or the receiver: an ambiguous symbol that leads to faulty encoding, a poor telephone connection, an inattentive receiver, faulty decoding, a prejudice, or a gesture that contradicts the words.

…and what appeared second — feedback

Feedback is all those actions of the receiver indicating that he has received and understood the message. Until it arrives, the sender has transmitted, not communicated. That is why the definition ends in "to reach a common understanding" — and why every measure for overcoming barriers ends with "ensure proper feedback".

Question — which element failed?

Case 15 · Hotel Amber Palace, Jaipur

Nandini Rathore telephoned the banquet manager from the airport and said, "Take care of the Sharma party." She meant transport, rooms and dinner until the family left Jaipur; he understood it as "escort them to their rooms", and the family waited ninety minutes for a car. The line was also breaking up.

Question 7 3 marks

Identify the element of the communication process that broke down, name the kind of noise involved, and state the one measure that would have prevented it.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. Decoding failedthe receiver converted the symbols into a different meaning from the one the sender intended.
  2. The noise was an unclarified assumption"take care of" is a semantic barrier — the poor telephone line is a second, separate noise, this one in the media.
  3. The measure — ensure proper feedbackhad she asked him to repeat what he would arrange, the gap would have surfaced in ten seconds.
"She meant transport, rooms and dinner until the family left Jaipur; he understood it as 'escort them to their rooms'…"

Formal communication

Definition — learn this wording

Formal communication flows through the official channels designed in the organisation chart. It may be oral or written, but is generally recorded and filed in the office.

Vertical — downward

Superior to subordinate. Examples: a notice to attend a meeting, an order to complete an assigned work, top management's guidelines passed down.

Vertical — upward

Subordinate to superior. Examples: an application for leave, a progress report, a request for a grant.

Horizontal / lateral

Between employees of the same cadre or between divisions — the production manager calling the marketing manager about a delivery schedule.

Case 16 · Deccan Autocomp, Pune

On 4 May the plant head circulated a signed notice putting the shutdown on 18 May (downward). The press-shop supervisor filed a written requisition for two spare dies (upward). The quality manager rang the production manager, his equal in rank, to agree the inspection sequence for the shutdown week (horizontal).

The five formal communication networks

Informal communication — the grapevine

Definition — learn this wording

Communication that takes place without following the formal lines of communication is informal communication. It is called the grapevine because it spreads throughout the organisation with its branches going out in all directions in utter disregard of the levels of authority.

Where it comes from

It arises out of the employees' need to exchange views that cannot be exchanged through formal channels — workers in the canteen discussing a superior's behaviour, or a rumour about transfers.

What it does

It spreads rapidly, it gets distorted, its source is hard to detect, and it generates rumours that can damage the work environment.

The examiner's line

An intelligent manager does not try to abolish the grapevine — it cannot be abolished. He makes use of its positive aspects and minimises the negative ones: he uses informal channels to release information and to sense his subordinates' reactions.

Case 17 · Hooghly Jute Mills, Kolkata

Three days before any circular was issued, the whole weaving shed already "knew" that the mill would go on a four-day week — the story had travelled from a clerk in the accounts room to the canteen and back. Two operators resigned on the strength of it. The circular, when it came, announced no such thing.

The four grapevine networks

Confusable pair — formal vs informal communication

Students routinely write…

"Informal communication is communication between friends, so it happens outside office hours." — No. The grapevine runs inside the organisation, during working hours, about organisational matters. What makes it informal is that it does not follow the official channels of the organisation chart — nothing to do with friendship or the clock.

Four bases. Note that "speed" and "reliability" move in opposite directions — that contrast is worth a mark on its own.
BasisFormal communicationInformal communication (grapevine)
Meaning Flows through the official channels designed in the organisation chart. Flows without following the formal lines, out of social relationships.
Authority levels Follows the scalar chain — upward, downward or horizontal. Disregards the levels of authority entirely; a clerk may tell a general manager.
Record Generally recorded and filed in the office; evidence exists. Not recorded; the source is difficult even to detect.
Speed and reliability Slower — but authentic and reliable. Very fast — but liable to distortion and rumour.

★ Challenge — too much of the wrong channel

Case 18 · Vaayu Systems, Chandigarh

In the Mohali office everybody approaches anybody for every small query. Nothing is put in writing, work is interrupted all day, and a client's unreleased pricing sheet reached a competitor through a lunch-table conversation.

Challenge 4 marks

What system should the manager adopt, and why? Name the network of formal communication that fits an office where a superior must control who talks to whom, and say what he should not attempt.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. Adopt formal communicationroute messages through the official channels designed in the organisation chart, oral or written but recorded and filed.
  2. Why — it restores efficiency and secrecyformal channels are slower but authentic; they end the all-day interruptions and give an audit trail for confidential information.
  3. The network that fits — the wheelall subordinates communicate through one superior, who acts as the hub, and are not allowed to talk among themselves.

Barriers to communication — the four families

Semantic barriers — the words themselves

Definition — learn this wording

Semantic barriers are problems and obstructions in the process of encoding and decoding a message into words or impressions. They arise from the use of wrong words, faulty translations and different interpretations.

  1. Badly expressed messageinadequate vocabulary, wrong words, omitted words.
  2. Symbols with different meanings"What is the value of this ring?" / "I value our friendship." — one word, three meanings.
  3. Faulty translationsa circular drafted in English and rendered badly into Hindi for the shop floor.
  4. Unclarified assumptions"Take care of our guest" — transport, food and accommodation, or just a lift to the hotel?
  5. Technical jargona specialist explaining to a non-specialist in the language of his own field.
  6. Body language and gesture decodingwhen what is said and what the body shows do not match, the message is wrongly perceived.
Case 19 · Deccan Autocomp, Pune

The design head e-mailed the press shop: "Hold the runout within 40 microns TIR on the pilot batch." The shop in-charge, a diploma holder with twenty years on the machines, did not know what TIR meant and did not ask. The batch was measured on the wrong axis.

Psychological barriers — the state of mind

Definition — learn this wording

Psychological or emotional barriers arise from the state of mind of the sender and the receiver. A worried person cannot communicate properly and an angry receiver cannot grasp the real meaning of the message.

  1. Premature evaluationthe receiver evaluates the meaning before the sender has finished, out of pre-conceived notions or prejudice.
  2. Lack of attentiona pre-occupied mind does not listen — the boss reading a file while an employee explains his problem.
  3. Loss by transmission and poor retentiona message passing through many levels loses accuracy, and people do not retain what they were not interested in.
  4. Distrustparties who do not believe each other cannot understand each other's message in its original sense.
Case 20 · Hotel Amber Palace, Jaipur

The housekeeping supervisor began explaining a new linen-tracking sheet to the GM. Two lines in, the GM said "I know, I know — this is the same idea that failed in 2023", and moved to the next agenda item while opening a bill file. The sheet was in fact a different design, and it was never adopted.

Two barriers, one paragraph

Premature evaluation — he judged before the sender finished. Lack of attention — the pre-occupied mind, opening a bill file. Both are psychological.

Organisational barriers — the system

Definition — learn this wording

Organisational barriers arise from the organisation structure, authority relationships, rules and regulations.

  1. Organisational policya policy that is not supportive of free flow — for example a highly centralised pattern in which people are not encouraged to communicate.
  2. Rules and regulationsrigid rules and cumbersome procedures; insisting on the prescribed channel causes delay.
  3. Statusthe superior's status creates a psychological distance; a status-conscious manager does not let subordinates speak freely.
  4. Complexity in the organisation structuretoo many managerial levels means more filtering points, so the message is delayed and distorted.
  5. Organisational facilitiesthe absence of meetings, suggestion boxes, complaint boxes and social gatherings blocks free communication.
Case 21 · Vaayu Systems, Chandigarh

A tester found a payment bug an hour before a release. The firm's rule is that all defects travel by written ticket through the team lead, then the project manager, then the delivery head. He was not permitted to telephone the delivery head directly, the ticket cleared three queues in six hours, and the build shipped with the bug in it.

Which one — and why not "personal"?

Rules and regulations — insisting on the prescribed channel causes delay, and three levels of routing is also complexity in the organisation structure. It is not a personal barrier: the tester was willing to speak and the delivery head willing to listen. The system stopped them, and it would have stopped anyone.

Status appears twice — and that is not an error

Status is an organisational barrier when it is the rank difference that silences the subordinate. It is a non-financial incentive when it is the ranking of a post being offered as a reward. Same word, two roles — read the case, not the word.

Personal barriers — the two people

Definition — learn this wording

Personal barriers arise from the personal factors of the sender and the receiver — that is, of the superior and of the subordinate.

On the superior's side

  1. Fear of challenge to authorityhe withholds a communication that might weaken his own position.
  2. Lack of confidence in subordinatesbelieving them incompetent, he never asks for their advice or opinion.

On the subordinate's side

  1. Unwillingness to communicatehe stays silent when he thinks speaking will damage his own interest.
  2. Lack of proper incentivesif a good suggestion earns no reward or appreciation, no more suggestions come.
Case 22 · Hooghly Jute Mills, Kolkata

A weaver worked out that re-sequencing the batching feed would save two hours a shift. He said nothing, because when a colleague made a suggestion in 2024 it was adopted, the department in-charge took the credit, and the colleague got not even a mention on the notice board.

Which one?

Lack of proper incentives — a personal barrier on the subordinate's side. Note it is not an organisational barrier: no rule, policy or level stopped him. His own calculation of what speaking was worth did.

Question 8 4 marks

  1. (a)At Vaayu Systems a leave request must pass four levels; by the time it reaches HR the developer has already taken the day.
  2. (b)A shift in-charge does not pass a worker's cost-saving idea upward because "the works manager will ask why I did not think of it".
  3. (c)The Jaipur hotel's safety circular was translated into Hindi by an agency and now instructs staff to "leave the fire" rather than "leave in case of fire".
  4. (d)A supervisor stops listening to a complaint after ten seconds because "these two always complain".
Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer — 4 marks, 4 points
  1. (a) Organisational barrier — rules and regulationsrigid procedure and the prescribed channel causing delay; four levels is also complexity in the structure.
  2. (b) Personal barrier — fear of challenge to authoritythe superior suppresses a communication he thinks will adversely affect his own position.
  3. (c) Semantic barrier — faulty translationthe translator was not proficient in both languages, so the meaning changed.
  4. (d) Psychological barrier — premature evaluationthe meaning was judged before the sender finished, out of a pre-conceived notion.

Notice how close (a) and (b) look. The test is the one from the diagram: (a) is the system — anyone's leave request would be delayed. (b) is the person — another in-charge would have passed it up.

Measures to overcome the barriers

1 · Clarify the ideas before communicating

Study and analyse the problem in depth so that it is clear to the sender first.

2 · Communicate according to the needs of the receiver

Adjust to the receiver's education and level of understanding.

3 · Consult others before communicating

Involve others in developing the plan for communication — it buys ready acceptance.

4 · Be aware of language, tone and content

The language must be understandable and must not offend the sentiments of the listener.

5 · Convey things of help and value to the listener

Know the receiver's interests and needs — a message that touches them evokes a response.

6 · Ensure proper feedback

Ask questions about the message conveyed and encourage the receiver to respond.

7 · Communicate for the present as well as the future

Meet existing commitments, but aim also at the future goals of the enterprise.

8 · Follow up communications

Review regularly whether the instruction is being implemented, and remove the hurdles.

9 · Be a good listener

Patient and attentive listening solves half the problems — and shows the subordinate that listening is happening.

The five traps this chapter sets

1 · Supervision — element or level?

As an element it is done by every manager at every level. As a level it is the post immediately above the worker. Read which the question wants.

2 · "Non-financial means it costs nothing"

It usually costs a great deal. Classify by the need satisfied — economic, or psychological / social / emotional.

3 · Motivation ≠ leadership

Motivation satisfies a need; leadership exerts personal influence. Motivating is one of the things a leader does.

4 · Democratic ≠ the group decides

The democratic leader consults, then decides. If the group decides, the style has slid into laissez faire.

5 · Barriers get listed, not classified

Four questions, in order: was it the word, the state of mind, the system, or the person?

Exam practice — six marks

Question 12 6 marks

"Discuss Maslow's Need Hierarchy theory of motivation." — Six marks, so six points. Plan them before you write.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer — the six points
  1. Basic physiological needshunger, thirst, shelter, sleep — satisfied organisationally by basic salary.
  2. Safety / security needssecurity and protection from physical and emotional harm — job security, stability of income, pension plans.
  3. Affiliation / belonging needsaffection, a sense of belongingness, acceptance and friendship — friendly work groups and team work.
  4. Esteem needsself-respect, autonomy, status, recognition and attention — a higher designation, praise, a recognition programme.
  5. Self-actualisation needsthe drive to become what one is capable of becoming — growth, self-fulfilment, achievement of goals; satisfied by challenging work with scope for creativity.
  6. The assumptions that hold it togetherbehaviour is based on needs; needs are satisfied in hierarchical order from the lowest upward; a person moves up only when the lower need is satisfied; and a satisfied need no longer motivates.

Question 13 6 marks

(a) Identify the leadership style in use and its effect. (b) Explain, using Maslow, why the ₹2,000 bonus failed. (c) Recommend two non-financial incentives, naming each in the exam's words.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. (a) The style is autocratic / authoritarianthe in-charge gives orders and expects obedience, authority is fully centralised in him and communication is one-way.
  2. Its effectemployees take no initiative and fear expressing opinions; the two best operators are leaving, which is employee turnover.
  3. (b) The unsatisfied need is esteem, not physiologicalwages and the canteen have already satisfied the physiological level, and a satisfied need no longer motivates — so more money reached a need nobody had.
  4. What the operators actually wantself-respect, autonomy, recognition and attention — being asked rather than told.
  5. (c) Employee participationinvolve them in the decisions related to them — put loom allocation before a works committee with operator members.
  6. (c) Employee recognition programmeacknowledgment with a show of appreciation — name the best line on the shed notice board each month.

Full case study 2 — two concepts collide

Case 25 · Hotel Amber Palace, Jaipur

Nandini Rathore circulates a signed note on 1 April: the banquet hall will close for renovation from 1 June. By 3 April the housekeeping floor "knows" that the hotel is being sold and that jobs will go — the story travelled from the linen room to the staff canteen, and each teller added a little. Six attendants apply elsewhere. On 8 April Nandini calls a full staff meeting, reads the note aloud, invites questions for an hour, and asks the union secretary to repeat back what the plan is.

Question 14 6 marks

(a) Name the two types of communication at work and identify which grapevine network the rumour followed. (b) Which need level did the rumour threaten? (c) Name three measures Nandini used on 8 April.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. (a) Formal communicationthe signed note circulated on 1 April flowed through the official channel and was recorded.
  2. (a) Informal communication — the grapevineit disregarded the levels of authority, spread rapidly and got distorted: a closure became a sale.
  3. (a) The network is the single strandthe story passed from one person to the next in a single sequence — linen room to canteen, each teller adding to it, which is why it is the most distorted of the four.
  4. (b) Safety and security needsthe rumour attacked certainty about future income and work, which is why six attendants applied elsewhere.
  5. (c) Measure one — clarify the ideas and communicate to the receiver's needshe read the actual note aloud to the people it affected.
  6. (c) Measures two and three — be a good listener, and ensure proper feedbackan hour of questions is attentive listening; asking the union secretary to repeat the plan back is feedback, which proves common understanding was reached.
"…the story travelled from the linen room to the staff canteen, and each teller added a little."

★ Full case study 3 — which element, and which style?

Case 26 · Deccan Autocomp, Pune

A German customer audit is due in nine days. Sanjay Kulkarni calls the four department heads, puts the audit checklist on the table and asks each to propose his own corrective plan; he adopts three of the four proposals and rewrites the fourth himself. He then tells the press-shop head that for the next nine days the shop will run exactly as he directs, with no deviation, because a single scrapped batch will fail the audit. He also announces that the four departments will jointly be given a day's outing if the audit is cleared.

Challenge 6 marks

Identify the two leadership styles used, say which situation justified each, name the element of directing behind the day's outing, and classify that incentive.

Are you ready for the answer? 🤔
Answer
  1. With the department heads — democratic / participativehe developed the action plan in consultation with subordinates and encouraged them to participate, then decided himself by rewriting the fourth plan.
  2. Why it fitsthe four heads are experienced and competent subordinates, which is the stated suitability of the democratic style.
  3. With the press shop — autocratic / authoritarianorders given, obedience expected, no deviation, communication one-way.
  4. Why it fitsa scrapped batch would fail the audit, so quick decisions and close control are essential — the appropriate technique for that situation.
  5. The outing is motivationthe element of directing that stimulates people to action by satisfying their needs; here the affiliation and esteem needs of a whole team.
  6. Classify it as a non-financial incentivethe firm pays for the outing, but the emphasis is on psychological and social satisfaction — it works on organisational climate, not on the pay packet.
"He then tells the press-shop head that for the next nine days the shop will run exactly as he directs, with no deviation…"
